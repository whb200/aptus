Leaner… and meaner?

How fourteen of the most important companies in the world changed recruiting—and redefined healthy growth.

The recruiting boom ended. The machinery did not return.

Corporate recruiting reached its post-pandemic high-water mark in 2022. Technology companies made the reversal visible, but the contraction was broader: by mid-2026, eleven of the fourteen companies in this sample employed fewer people in talent acquisition than they had four years earlier.

Recruiting shrank faster than the companies it served.

At twelve of the fourteen companies, total workforce was flat or larger by FY2025—even as TA contracted at most of them. The restructuring was therefore not simply a response to fewer employees. It was a management bet that less recruiting capacity could support the same institution.

A measure of output—not an HR score.

To compare companies with radically different economics, this analysis uses gross profit per employee. It is a cleaner cross-company lens than revenue per employee, while still requiring care: pricing, mix, margins, and capital intensity can all move the result.

Why not revenue?

A dollar of software and a dollar of retail resale do not carry the same economics. Gross profit strips out more of that structural difference.

What it reveals

How much value remains after direct costs, relative to the size of the workforce supporting the business.

What it cannot prove

It measures a business outcome, not isolated labor quality. Pricing, product mix, and margins can move the number without anyone becoming more effective.

Bank exception

JPMorgan is shown on net revenue per employee because banks do not report a comparable cost-of-revenue line.

How the people who hire people were counted.

Why TA is worth measuring
“The TA org chart is a confession.” A recruiting organization is a forward commitment: a bet on next year’s hiring demand, paid for with this year’s payroll. That is why this analysis treats recruiting capacity as an operating signal, not merely an HR cost.

Measures

TA headcount. Point-in-time censuses for June 2022 and June 2026 from Coresignal’s employee dataset, using nested queries that bind employer, title, and dates to the same job entry. Counts above the API’s 1,000-record page were resolved by partitioning the ID space and deduplicating the union. Current counts were cross-validated against People Data Labs.

Workforce and financials. Total employees, revenue, cost of revenue, and free cash flow come from 10-K filings for fiscal 2022 and the most recent completed fiscal year. Fiscal-year mappings differ for Walmart, Home Depot, and Nvidia.

Output measure. Gross profit per employee equals revenue minus cost of revenue, divided by year-end headcount on the same basis in both years. JPMorgan uses total net revenue per employee. Incremental gross profit per marginal employee is calculated only where workforce change exceeds 3% in absolute terms.

What could make this wrong

  1. Correlation is not causation. TA cuts and gains in gross profit per employee may share a third driver rather than a direct relationship.
  2. Observed declines are likely floors. Stale profiles inflate both census dates and generally understate contraction.
  3. Outsourcing is partly invisible. RPO substitution can move recruiters off the company’s apparent payroll.
  4. Levels vary by vendor. The analysis relies more heavily on within-instrument change than on absolute cross-vendor counts.
  5. Fiscal years do not align. Annual comparisons are approximate across companies with different year ends.
