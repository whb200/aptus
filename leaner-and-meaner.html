Leaner—and Meaner?

Fourteen leading companies cut recruiting capacity far faster than they cut employment. Most kept growing. Their choices reveal six competing models of healthy growth.

The hiring machine was built for a world that ended.

In the free-money years, workers quit at record rates, and those quitters needed to be backfilled. Growth was the metric markets paid for so companies industrialized their appetite, building recruiting functions of unprecedented scale. At the peak, Meta staffed one recruiter for every twenty employees. Then rates rose and the logic inverted. Capital became expensive, boards began rewarding discipline over expansion, and CEOs said the quiet part at full volume: fewer dollars would go to headcount, and the fat would be cut deliberately. Workers, for their part, stopped moving, out of caution, or because the doors were closing, or both; the honest answer is that nobody can fully separate the two. What's beyond dispute is where the knife fell first: on the people whose job was to add people. And, for all of the clamor around tech downsizing, our study of the “Magnificent Seven” versus a more established blue-chip cohort (our “Steady Seven”) reveals that the pain cut across sectors, even if it wasn’t distributed evenly.

Growth continued. The hiring model changed.

The cuts were surgical, not general. Companies weren't shrinking themselves; most in this sample held or grew total headcount even as they took recruiting down by a third or more. What they were exiting was a particular model of hiring: the standing army. That model kept thousands of full-time recruiters on payroll to fight for candidates in a seller's market — outbound sourcing, courtship, competing offers. When the market flipped, the expensive half of that work simply evaporated. With hundreds of qualified applicants arriving for every opening, most companies no longer needed to hunt. They needed a filter. So the new model keeps a thin permanent intake layer, pushes screening onto software and hiring managers to the extent possible, redeploys internally instead of buying externally, and treats recruiting capacity as something you rent back — through agencies and RPO — if demand ever returns. It helped that the recruiter was the safest possible cut: eliminating one touches no product, no customer, and no revenue; it only reduces the company's capacity to grow later. In 2023, later was a problem for later.

The two exceptions prove the mechanism. Nvidia and Caterpillar — the sample's fastest capital compounders — added recruiters, because their constraint was never demand for jobs but supply of scarce people. When you're still hunting, you keep the hunting party.

Now, a few years later, the question is still begging to be answered: could these companies who promised to do more with less actually do it?

A positive signal, not a victory lap.

Everything so far describes what companies did. This is where we ask whether it worked, or at least, whether it worked on the terms the companies themselves set. “Do more with less” was the promise, offered loudly and often. Gross profit per employee is the closest thing to a scoreboard for that promise: how much value the business generates, after direct costs, per person employed to generate it. The answer, at the median company in this sample: up meaningfully, and up most where the cuts went deepest. The companies that took recruiting down by half or more posted productivity gains several times larger than the companies that barely touched it.

Read that carefully before celebrating, because the number is a scoreboard for the business, not for the labor decisions alone. Pricing moved these figures. Product mix moved them. An AI boom moved some of them enormously. And the companies that cut hardest were often cutting from 2022's depressed base, which flatters their rebound. What the data supports is narrower and more interesting than triumph: the companies that suppressed their appetite for headcount got leaner and healthier on paper, in the same window. Whether the leanness caused the health, rode along with it, or merely signaled a confidence that was already there — that's the question the six patterns below let you examine case by case.

Six models, six tradeoffs, no universal prescription.

Nvidia (+523%) is pinned to the top edge. Select any company for the underlying arithmetic.

Sort these fourteen companies by what they did rather than what they are, and the familiar taxonomy dissolves. The tech-versus-incumbent line, the one every 2023 headline was organized around, turns out to explain almost nothing: Disney cut deeper than Microsoft, Walmart matched it, and the two companies still adding recruiters are an AI lab and a bulldozer manufacturer. What emerges instead are six distinct answers to the same question every board was quietly asking: how much capacity to grow are we willing to pay for while we wait to find out if we'll need it? Each answer is a real strategy with a real bill tradeoff, some payable now, some deferred. None of them is a verdict, because the experiment isn't over. What follows is each pattern, its logic, its cost, and the question it leaves open.

Every archetype on this page is an open bet. The Hard Reset is betting that hiring muscle can regrow faster than it atrophied. The Lean Growth Engine is betting that today's conditions — torrential inbound, docile and mostly wanted attrition, enhanced tech solutions — are the new physics rather than a weather cycle. The Rebalancers are betting they cut fat and not reach; the Steady-State Operators, that continuity beats reconstruction; the Capacity Builders, that scarce talent is worth carrying capacity for before the demand is confirmed.

What settles these bets is the thaw. A frozen market flatters every lean model: scarce openings and anxious workers mean candidates arrive in torrents, and filtering a torrent is cheap. It was generating candidates in a candidate-obsessed market that justified the standing army. The day attrition normalizes and the inbound torrent thins, we find out which companies cut a bloated function and which ones sold the plumbing. And that points at the question this study can pose but not answer: did the freeze make recruiting organizations redundant, or merely make them look redundant? The honest reading of fourteen companies is that nobody knows yet — including, one suspects, the companies themselves.

In the meantime, the data suggests a different use for these numbers than judgment. A recruiting organization is the one line item that encodes what a company believes about its own future: its size is a confession of expected growth, its composition a statement of which constraint — demand or talent — management actually fears. So one is wise to watch a TA org chart the way you'd watch insider buying: not because it's always right, but because it's what they do with their own money when they think the analysts aren't looking.

What the data can—and cannot—tell us.

Measures

TA headcount. Point-in-time censuses for June 2022 and June 2026 from Coresignal’s employee dataset, using nested queries that bind employer, title, and dates to the same job entry. Counts above the API’s 1,000-record page were resolved by partitioning the ID space and deduplicating the union. Current counts were cross-validated against People Data Labs.

Workforce and financials. Total employees, revenue, cost of revenue, and free cash flow come from 10-K filings for fiscal 2022 and the most recent completed fiscal year. Fiscal-year mappings differ for Walmart, Home Depot, and Nvidia.

Output measure. Gross profit per employee equals revenue minus cost of revenue, divided by year-end headcount on the same basis in both years. JPMorgan uses total net revenue per employee. Incremental gross profit per marginal employee is calculated only where workforce change exceeds 3% in absolute terms.

What could make this wrong

  1. Correlation is not causation. TA cuts and gains in gross profit per employee may share a third driver rather than a direct relationship.
  2. Observed declines are likely floors. Stale profiles inflate both census dates and generally understate contraction.
  3. Outsourcing is partly invisible. RPO substitution can move recruiters off the company’s apparent payroll.
  4. Levels vary by vendor. The analysis relies more heavily on within-instrument change than on absolute cross-vendor counts.
  5. Fiscal years do not align. Annual comparisons are approximate across companies with different year ends.
